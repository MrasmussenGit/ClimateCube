Use the trend to see heating, cooling, and how quickly the room responds to outdoor or HVAC changes. Relative humidity is how full the air is with moisture at its current temperature. Roughly 30–60% is commonly comfortable. Sustained levels above about 60% can encourage condensation, dust mites, and mold, while very low levels can feel dry. Because relative humidity changes when temperature changes, also compare dew point and longer trends. Barometric pressure is the weight of the atmosphere, reported in hectopascals. Indoor and outdoor pressure are usually almost the same; broad rises and falls mainly reflect changing weather. This sensor does not measure room pressurization, airflow, ventilation quality, or whether combustion appliances are drafting safely. Use outdoor dew point for a more direct view of moisture and compare this series with indoor humidity to study ventilation and moisture movement. Dew point describes the actual moisture content of outdoor air: it is the temperature at which that air would become saturated and condensation could begin. Higher dew points feel more humid. When an indoor surface is colder than the surrounding air's dew point, condensation can form; surface temperature is not measured here.
